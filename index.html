How Supplement Brands Change After Being Acquired

Supplement brands change hands often. A small brand with a loyal following is bought by a larger company, and the product that loyal customers loved starts to change. The name stays the same, the label stays the same, and the marketing stays the same, but the formula, the price, the testing and the customer service can all shift quietly in the background. Knowing what usually changes after an acquisition helps you judge whether the product you liked is still the product on the shelf.

The first thing that usually changes is the formula. A larger owner will often look for ways to reduce the cost of making the product, and the formula is where the savings are found. An expensive ingredient may be replaced with a cheaper form. A dose may be reduced. A proprietary blend may appear where fully disclosed amounts used to be. None of these changes is announced on the label, because the label only has to list what is in the product, not what used to be in it. A buyer who compares the current label with an older one will sometimes find the formula has changed substantially while the product name has not changed at all.

The second thing that usually changes is the manufacturing. A larger owner may move production to its own facility, or to a cheaper contract manufacturer, to consolidate its operations. The new facility may follow different procedures, use different suppliers, and test to a different standard. The product may be made in a different country, with different raw materials, under different oversight. The label will still say the product is made to the same standard, because the standard is the brand's own claim rather than an independent certification, and the brand controls what it claims.

The third thing that usually changes is the testing. A small brand that tested every batch may be replaced by a larger operation that tests a sample of batches, or that relies on the manufacturer's own testing rather than independent verification. Testing is expensive, and it is one of the first costs a new owner looks at. The change is invisible to the buyer, because the label still says "third-party tested" and the website still shows a certificate. The certificate may simply be older, or may describe a different batch, or may come from a laboratory with a narrower scope than the one the brand used before.

The fourth thing that usually changes is the price. An acquisition is often followed by a price rise, justified as reflecting the new owner's investment in the brand. Sometimes the price rise comes with a larger bottle, or a new formula, or a new claim. Sometimes it comes with nothing at all except a new owner. A price rise after an acquisition is not proof that the product got worse, but it is a prompt to check whether the formula, the dose and the testing changed at the same time as the price.

The fifth thing that usually changes is the customer service. A small brand with a named team that answers emails personally may be replaced by a larger operation with a call centre, a ticketing system and a script. The guarantee may stay the same on paper, but the experience of claiming it can change completely. A refund that used to take a day may take a month. A question that used to be answered by someone who knew the product may be answered by someone reading from a screen. The product is the same, but the company behind it is not.

A practical way to check whether a brand has changed after an acquisition is to compare the label today with the label from a year ago, and to ask the brand directly what has changed. An acquisition is not a verdict. Some brands improve under new ownership, with better testing and better manufacturing. But the buyer who assumes nothing changed because the name stayed the same is the buyer most likely to be surprised. The name is the part of the product that changes least. Everything else is negotiable.
